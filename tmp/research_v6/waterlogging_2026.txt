Abstract
Industrial hemp is often considered sensitive to waterlogging; however, empirical ev-
idence remains scarce. This study evaluated the effects of waterlogging duration and
timing on the physiology, growth, and development of hemp under controlled conditions.
Two glasshouse experiments were conducted using industrial hemp (Cannabis sativaL.)
cv. Ferimon 12. The first examined five waterlogging durations: 0 (control), 3, 6, 12, and
24 days, imposed at the eight true leaf pair stage. The second examined 3- and 6-day
waterlogging events imposed at the six true leaf pair and flower initiation stages. In Ex-
periment 1, waterlogging beyond 6 days caused severe damage, and plants died after
12 days of continuous waterlogging. Plants subjected to 3–6 days of waterlogging survived,
but photosynthetic activity declined by 40–80%, biomass by 18–55%, and seed yield by
16–48% relative to the control, with the greatest reductions occurring after 6 days of wa-
terlogging. Experiment 2 showed that plant sensitivity varied with developmental stage,
with plants exhibiting greater susceptibility at the six true leaf pair stage compared to the
flower initiation stage. The dual-purpose hemp cultivar Ferimon 12 was highly sensitive to
waterlogging, particularly during early vegetative growth, with even short-term saturation
impairing photosynthesis, stomatal conductance, growth and yield. These findings charac-
terise the physiological limits of this cultivar under controlled conditions and demonstrate
that developmental stage influences the severity of waterlogging impacts.

1. Introduction
Industrial hemp (Cannabis sativaL.) is one of the world’s oldest cultivated crops and
has been widely adopted due to its versatility. It is used in textiles, bioplastics, construction
materials, nutritional products, and medicinal purposes, with cannabidiol rich varieties
valued for their therapeutic properties. The growing demand for hemp-derived products,
together with the crop’s broad adaptability and the easing of cultivation restrictions in
many regions, has renewed interest in understanding how environmental conditions and
agronomic management practices influence hemp growth and productivity.
Hemp requires adequate moisture during establishment, particularly within the first
six weeks [1]. However, excessive water can reduce growth, depress yield, and even cause
crop failure. Waterlogging induces prolonged rootzone saturation and oxygen deficiency,

impairing root respiration, nutrient uptake, and plant development [ 2]. Consequently,
well-drained soils and the avoidance of prolonged saturation are essential for optimal
hemp growth. Under anaerobic conditions, plant roots shift from aerobic respiration to
less efficient fermentation pathways, resulting in lower energy production [ 3] and the
accumulation of toxic metabolites that damage root cells [3]. Root hypoxia or anoxia further
triggers physiological responses such as increased abscisic acid (ABA) levels, leading
to stomatal closure, reduced water and nutrient uptake, decreased photosynthetic rates,
and ultimately suppressed growth and wilting [ 4,5]. Waterlogging further decreases
chlorophyll content and chlorophyll fluorescence, indicating impaired photosynthetic
performance [6,7].
Numerous studies have investigated the effects of water stress (drought) on the physi-
ology, growth, and yield of industrial hemp [8–15]. In contrast, research on waterlogging in
hemp remains limited [16], with much of the available evidence being anecdotal and lack-
ing systematic evaluation. Although waterlogging effects have been extensively studied
in other crops such as cotton, wheat, and barley [17–21], comparatively few studies have
addressed how hemp responds to waterlogging conditions [16].
The impact of waterlogging on crop performance varies with crop sensitivity, the
duration of waterlogging and the growth stage at which it occurs [ 22]. Kumar et al.
reported that a short duration of waterlogging at the vegetative stage of industrial hemp in
a field trial reduced photosynthetic traits; however, these traits recovered by the end of the
growth stage [16]. In contrast, waterlogging during critical reproductive phases has been
shown in other crops, such as cotton, to significantly reduce plant height, leaf area, stem
diameter, and yield [17,21,23,24]. Wang et al. found that waterlogging effects increase with
duration and are highly stage-dependent in cotton, with the flowering stage being the most
sensitive, where even 2 days of waterlogging can cause irreversible damage, while plants
at early growth stages can recover from short-term stress [21]. The flowering stage is highly
sensitive to waterlogging because reproductive development requires substantial energy
and nutrient supply, which is compromised by oxygen deficiency, reduced root function,
decreased photosynthesis, and hormonal imbalances under stress, ultimately leading to
flower abortion and irreversible yield loss [25].
While short term exposure may allow some degree of recovery, prolonged waterlog-
ging generally results in substantial reductions in biomass accumulation and final yield
in other crops [17,21,24,26,27]. Beegum et al. reported that waterlogging beyond 4–6 days
initiates significant growth reduction, with severe physiological and biomass losses occur-
ring after 8–10 days, and maximum damage observed at 12–14 days in cotton [17]. Milroy
and Bange also observed that photosynthesis in cotton declined significantly after 72 h
of waterlogging but recovered once soil oxygen levels improved [ 23]. They also found
that plants showed some acclimation, with photosynthesis becoming less sensitive to low
oxygen at later growth stages [23].
In a previous study, Kumar et al. showed that excess water causing waterlogging
during the vegetative and flower initiation stages reduced photosynthesis by 20–30% and
limited hemp growth in field conditions [ 16]. This field study established the impact
of contrasting water regimes on crop performance. However, a precise evaluation of
waterlogging effects requires controlled conditions; therefore, the present experiment was
conducted in a pot-based controlled system. Containerized cultivation is widely used
in both research and commercial production of cannabis, including medicinal cannabis
systems [28–30]. Cannabis and hemp are taxonomically the same species,Cannabis sativa
L., differing mainly in cannabinoid profile and end use applications. Owing to their
similar growth physiology and cultivation practices, including containerised production

systems, the present study has direct relevance to both medicinal cannabis and industrial
hemp industries.
Accordingly, this study aims to address the existing knowledge gap by systematically
evaluating the effects of waterlogging imposed at different growth stages and durations
on hemp grown under controlled glasshouse conditions, with a focus on plant survival,
photosynthesis, growth, phenology, and yield.
2. Materials and Methods
Two glasshouse experiments were conducted to evaluate the effects of waterlogging
on hemp growth and physiological responses. The first experiment assessed plant tolerance
to different durations of waterlogging, while the second experiment was further refined
based on initial findings to examine plant responses to moderate waterlogging imposed at
different growth stages.
Both experiments used a single genotype grown in a uniform substrate to ensure
consistency and minimise genetic and edaphic variability. The use of a substrate also
allowed precise control of waterlogging duration and timing by reducing physical and
chemical heterogeneity typical of field soils. Using a uniform substrate minimised vari-
ability associated with soil physical and chemical properties, allowing plant responses to
be compared across waterlogging treatments under controlled conditions. This controlled
approach aligns with the study’s objective of establishing a foundational understanding of
the physiological and morphological responses of hemp to waterlogging.
2.1. Experimental Location and Growth Conditions
The experiments were conducted in a glasshouse at the Horticulture Research Centre
at the University of Tasmania, Hobart, Australia. The first experiment was conducted from
February to June 2019 and the second experiment from August to December 2019.
Daylength was maintained at 13 h 40 min to delay flowering [31], using natural day-
light supplemented, when required, with two 400 W mercury vapour lamps providing
approximately 40–80 µmol m−2 s−1 photosynthetically active radiation (PAR). Supplemen-
tal lighting duration was adjusted seasonally to maintain the target photoperiod.
Glasshouse temperatures were monitored using a Tiny Tag data logger. Temperatures
were generally maintained through ventilation at an average maximum of ~25 ◦C and
minimum of ~15 ◦C, which are within the optimal range for hemp growth. However, a few
higher temperature events (up to ~31 ◦C) were recorded due to occasional environmental
fluctuations; these instances were infrequent.
The monoecious hemp cultivar Ferimon 12 was selected for this study due to its
uniform growth habit and widespread use in industrial hemp production, which improves
the relevance of the findings. The use of a single variety further minimised genotypic
variability and enabled a clearer interpretation of treatment effects, ensuring that observed
differences in growth, physiology, and yield were primarily attributable to waterlogging
duration and timing rather than genetic differences.
Seeds were sown in 15 L polypropylene bags (24 cm height) filled with a substrate
consisting of a 1:1:1 mix of peat moss, perlite, and vermiculite. The peat–perlite–vermiculite
substrate was selected because of its uniform physical properties and rapid drainage under
non-waterlogged conditions, allowing the onset and removal of rootzone saturation to be
precisely controlled during treatment application. A substrate bulk density of 0.13 g cm−3
was calculated as the average of the reported bulk densities of peat moss (0.12 g cm −3),
perlite (0.08 g cm−3), and vermiculite (0.19 g cm−3) in the 1:1:1 (v/v/v) mixture and was
used for unit conversions. This estimate falls within the reported dry bulk density range of

0.08–0.14 g cm−3 for peat–perlite substrates [32]. The substrate pH was adjusted to 6.0–6.5
by incorporating dolomite at 36.2 g kg−1, providing Ca and Mg while buffering acidity.
Basal nutrients were applied prior to sowing by adding a controlled-release fertiliser
(Osmocote, 7.7 g kg −1, N:P:K ratio 15:4:9 plus micronutrients) in combination with a
micronutrient blend (Micromax, 8.5 g kg−1, Fe 6%w/w, Mn 2.5%w/w, Zn 1%w/w, Cu 0.5%
w/w, B 0.1%w/w, Mo 0.05%w/w), applied according to manufacturer recommendations
to meet nutritional requirements.
Four seeds were sown per pot at ~3 cm depth and thinned to one plant per pot
after emergence.
2.2. T reatments
2.2.1. Experiment 1
Waterlogging treatments began at 43 Days After Sowing (DAS) (eight true leaf pair veg-
etative stage). Before treatment, all plants received overhead sprinkler irrigation (19 L h−1)
for 8 min per day, applied in three intervals. Following treatment initiation, control plants
continued under the same irrigation regime, while waterlogged plants were maintained
under continuous inundation for the duration of their respective waterlogging treatments.
A randomised complete block design was used with six replicates and five waterlog-
ging durations: 0 (control), 3, 6, 12, and 24 days. Waterlogging was imposed by placing
individual 15 L pots into 50 L containers and maintaining the water level approximately
2.5 cm above the substrate surface throughout the treatment period. This ensured com-
plete saturation of the potting media and restricted gas exchange between the root zone
and atmosphere, creating conditions conducive to rootzone hypoxia. Water levels were
checked daily and adjusted as required to maintain consistent inundation. Following each
waterlogging period, pots were drained for 24 h without irrigation, after which normal
irrigation resumed.
2.2.2. Experiment 2
Similar to Experiment 1, before treatment, all plants received overhead sprinkler irriga-
tion (19 L h−1) for 8 min per day, applied in three intervals. Following treatment initiation,
control plants continued under the same irrigation regime, while waterlogged plants were
maintained under continuous inundation for the duration of their respective waterlogging
treatments. Waterlogging was imposed using the same protocol as Experiment 1, with pots
placed in 50 L containers and water maintained approximately 2.5 cm above the substrate
surface for the duration of each treatment.
A randomised complete block design was used with five replicates and three waterlog-
ging durations: 0 (control), 3, and 6 days. Waterlogging was applied at two growth stages:
six true leaf pair vegetative stage (35 DAS, GS 1012) and flower initiation stage (49 DAS, GS
2301). Following each waterlogging period, pots were drained for 24 h without irrigation,
after which normal irrigation resumed.
The treatments therefore included: 0-day waterlogging (control), 3-day waterlogging
at the six true leaf pair stage (3 VE), 6-day waterlogging at six true leaf pair vegetative
stage (6 VE), 3-day waterlogging at flower initiation stage (3 FI), and 6-day waterlogging at
flower initiation stage (6 FI).
2.3. Measurements
Volumetric water content of the substrate was recorded weekly at depths of 4.2, 12.5,
and 20.8 cm using an Acclima TDR-315 sensor (Acclima, Meridian, US). Values were
summed to provide an integrated measure of substrate water content. Additional measure-
ments were taken during waterlogging periods as required to monitor and maintain the
target waterlogging conditions.

From 4 DAS (after seedling emergence), plant development was monitored to calculate
thermal time to flower initiation (first visible pistils), end of flowering (95% male flowers
withered), and seed maturity (90% hard seeds). Thermal time was calculated using daily
maximum and minimum temperatures and a base temperature (Tb) of 5.7 ◦C, adopted
from the base temperature reported for leaf appearance in fibre hemp under controlled-
environment conditions [33]. Growth stages followed Mediavilla et al. [34].
Chlorophyll content was measured at 11:00 am on the two youngest fully expanded
leaves using a SPAD meter (Apogee Instruments, Logan, UT, USA). Chlorophyll fluo-
rescence (Fv/Fm) was measured after 30 min dark adaptation using a phase amplitude
modulated (PAM) fluorometer (OS-30 chlorophyll fluorometer, Opti Sciences, Hudson, NH,
USA) on the two youngest fully expanded leaves to assess PSII efficiency.
Net CO 2 assimilation rate (Pn), transpiration rate (Tr), and stomatal conductance
(Gs) were measured weekly using a Li-Cor 6400XT infrared gas analyser (IRGA; Li-Cor
Biosciences, Lincoln, US). Before measurement, plants were exposed to full sunlight for 1 h
near midday. Measurements were taken on the two youngest fully expanded leaves. The
IRGA chamber maintained leaf temperature at 20◦C, CO2 concentration at 400µmol mol −1,
CO2 flow rate at 400 µmol s−1 and PAR at 1500 µmol m−2 s−1. Photosynthetic water use
efficiency (PWUE) was calculated as Pn/Tr [35].
Plant height and stem diameter were measured weekly (for Experiment 1) and at
harvest (for Experiment 1 and Experiment 2). Plant height was measured from the substrate
level to the growing tip of the plant using a tape measure. Stem diameter was measured
using a Vernier calliper at the mid-point between the first node and the cotyledonary node.
Plants were harvested at the end of the seed maturity; dead and senesced leaves
were excluded. In Experiment 1, control and 3-day treatments were harvested at 142 DAS,
and the 6-day treatment at 138 DAS. In Experiment 2, control and 3 VE treatments were
harvested at 146 DAS. Waterlogged plants matured earlier: 6 VE plants were harvested at
139 DAS (with two plants dying before maturity), and 6 FI plants at 133 DAS.
At each harvest, fresh weight and leaf area were recorded. Bark proportion was
determined from stem sections. Stem, leaf, and reproductive tissues were separated and
oven dried at 60 ◦C for 48 h to determine dry yield. Seeds were separated, cleaned and
weighed, and total seed yield (g plant−1) and thousand seed weight (TSW) were calculated
for each treatment.
3. Data Analysis
Repeatedly measured variables, including chlorophyll content, chlorophyll fluores-
cence (Fv/Fm), net CO2 assimilation rate (Pn), transpiration rate (Tr), stomatal conductance
(Gs), plant height and stem diameter were analysed using the PROC MIXED procedure in
SAS version 9.4. The model included treatment, sampling time, and the treatment × time
interaction as fixed effects. Individual plants were considered the experimental units and
specified as subjects in the repeated-measures analysis to account for correlations among
observations collected from the same plant over time. Type III F-tests were used to evaluate
fixed effects. When significant treatment × time interactions were detected (p ≤ 0.05),
treatment means were compared within sampling dates using Tukey-adjusted multiple
comparisons. Plants that died during the experiment contributed data up to the time of
mortality, after which observations were treated as missing. PROC MIXED accommodated
missing repeated observations without requiring data imputation. Individual plants (n= 6
per treatment in Experiment 1;n= 5 per treatment in Experiment 2) were considered the
experimental units and specified as subjects in the repeated-measures analysis.
Data from destructive measurements were analysed using analysis of variance
(ANOVA) to evaluate the effects of waterlogging on plant responses. The sample size

(n= 5–6 replicates per treatment) was selected based on common practice in controlled-
environment and glasshouse studies, as well as logistical constraints associated with the
experimental setup. While a formal a priori power analysis was not performed, the study
detected significant treatment effects across several key response variables.
In Experiment 1, which included only waterlogging duration treatments (0-control, 3,
6, 12, and 24 days), treatment effects were assessed using one way ANOVA.
In Experiment 2, treatment means for all five treatments (0 days (control), 3 VE,
6 VE, 3 FI, and 6 FI) were first compared using one-way ANOVA to evaluate overall
treatment differences relative to the 0 days (control). To specifically examine the effects
of waterlogging duration and growth stage, a separate two-way ANOVA was conducted
using only waterlogging treatments (3 VE, 6 VE, 3 FI, and 6 FI). Waterlogging duration
(3 and 6 days) and growth stage (vegetative and flower initiation) were included as fixed
factors. The 0 days (control) was excluded from the factorial analysis because it could not
be assigned to either a waterlogging duration or a growth-stage treatment level.
Statistical significance was determined using Type III F tests atp ≤ 0.05. When
interactions were significant, treatment means were separated using Tukey’s HSD test.
For variables with non-significant interactions, main effects were interpreted and means
compared using Tukey’s HSD test.
4. Results
4.1. Experiment 1
4.1.1. Volumetric Water Content of the Substrate
Total volumetric water content in the control treatment remained stable throughout the
experiment. Waterlogging increased substrate water content by approximately 0.0417 L L−1
in the 3-day treatment, 0.0625 L L −1 in the 6- and 12-day treatments, and 0.083 L L −1 in
the 24-day treatment. Plants subjected to 12- and 24 days of waterlogging died by 66 DAS
(23 days after waterlogging). After waterlogging ceased, water content declined across all
treatments. Differences in substrate water content among treatments were minimal both
before and after the waterlogging events. (Figure S1).
4.1.2. Leaf Chlorophyll, CO2 Exchange, Stomatal Conductance and PWUE
Relative chlorophyll content, Pn, Tr, Gs and PWUE exhibited significant treat-
ment × time interactions (p< 0.05), indicating that responses to waterlogging differed
among treatments over time, particularly following treatment imposition and during the
recovery phase. Relative chlorophyll content, Pn, Tr, Gs and PWUE showed contrasting
temporal patterns among waterlogging treatments (Figure 1; Figure S2a,b). Treatment
differences were small prior to waterlogging but became apparent shortly after treatment
imposition (approximately 53 DAS) and increased during the subsequent recovery pe-
riod. Control plants maintained the highest values throughout the experiment, whereas
plants subjected to 3 and 6 days of waterlogging showed varying rates of recovery follow-
ing drainage.
Plants exposed to 3 days of waterlogging showed an initial decline in measured
variables but recovered rapidly after drainage, subsequently following a pattern broadly
similar to the control. In contrast, plants exposed to 6 days of waterlogging exhibited a
more prolonged reduction in relative chlorophyll content, Pn, Tr and Gs, with recovery
becoming evident only from approximately 74 DAS onwards. These variables gradually
increased thereafter and, by the end of the crop cycle, approached values observed in the
control and 3-day treatments. However, PWUE remained lower than the control and 3-day
treatments for most of the remainder of the crop cycle (Figure 1c).

4.1.3. Crop Phenology
Waterlogging duration did not affect the calendar and thermal time required for flower
initiation. The average thermal time for flower initiation was 662.2 ◦Cd. However, the
duration from flower initiation to the end of flowering was significantly shorter (p< 0.05)
in the 6-day waterlogging treatment (343 ◦Cd) compared with the control and 3-day
treatments. All plants in the 12- and 24-day treatments died shortly after flowering, whereas
plants in the other treatments continued to grow. The thermal time from the end of
flowering to seed maturity was consistent across the control, 3-day, and 6-day treatments,
with no significant differences (Table S1).
4.1.4. Plant Height and Stem Diameter
In the control, plant height and stem diameter increased steadily until flowering
(~66 DAS) and then plateaued. The 3-day waterlogging treatment caused a temporary
slowdown of growth, with full recovery by ~94 DAS. In contrast, plants waterlogged for
6 days experienced a prolonged growth setback and only partial recovery, resulting in
reduced final height and diameter compared with both the control and 3-day treatments.
Plants subjected to 12- and 24-day waterlogging showed irreversible growth suppression
and died by 66 DAS (Figure 2).

4.1.5. Specific Leaf Area (SLA), Dry Biomass, Stalk Yield and Seed Yield
Complete mortality occurred in the 12-day and 24-day waterlogging treatments fol-

and 6-day waterlogging treatments survived to seed maturity and were included in har-
vest assessments.
At harvest, SLA declined significantly as waterlogging duration increased from 0
to 6 days (p< 0.05). Similarly, leaf, stem, and total aboveground dry weights (TADW)
decreased with longer waterlogging, with the 6-day treatment producing significantly
lower values than both the control and 3-day treatments (p< 0.05) (Table 1).

Waterlogging had no significant effect on bark proportion at seed maturity. However,

4.2. Experiment 2
4.2.1. Volumetric Water Content of the Substrate
Water content in the control treatment (0-day) remained relatively consistent through-
out the experiment. In the waterlogging treatments, substrate water content increased by
~0.083 L L−1 following waterlogging and then declined after the waterlogging conditions
were removed. Similar to Experiment 1, differences in water content among treatments
were minimal both before and after the waterlogging events (Figure S3).
4.2.2. Leaf Chlorophyll, CO2 Exchange, Stomatal Conductance and PWUE
Relative chlorophyll content, F v/Fm, net CO 2 assimilation rate (Pn), transpiration
rate (Tr), stomatal conductance (Gs), and PWUE were all significantly influenced by the
treatment × time interaction (p< 0.05), indicating that the temporal response differed
among waterlogging treatments. Following the initial decline during waterlogging, plants
subjected to 3-day waterlogging (3 VE and 3 FI) recovered rapidly and subsequently
followed trajectories similar to the control for relative chlorophyll content, Fv/Fm, Pn, Tr
and Gs (Figure 3a–c; Figure S4a,b). In contrast, plants exposed to 6 days of waterlogging
(6 VE and 6 FI) exhibited more pronounced reductions and slower recovery, resulting in
significant differences from the control during the early post-waterlogging period before
converging later in the season (Figure 3a–c; Figure S4a,b).

Flower initiation occurred 7 days earlier (p< 0.05) in 6 VE plants, which required
517 ◦Cd of thermal time compared with the other treatments. The 6 FI plants progressed

4.2.4. Specific Leaf Area (SLA), Dry Biomass, Plant Height, Stem Diameter, Stalk Yield and
Seed Yield
Compared with the 0 days (control), all waterlogging treatments reduced one or
more growth and yield parameters at final harvest (Table 2). The greatest reductions
occurred in the 6 VE treatment, where SLA, total above-ground dry weight, stalk yield,
and seed yield were reduced by approximately 36%, 68%, 83%, and 63%, respectively,
relative to the control. Plant height and stem diameter were also reduced by 58% and
53%, respectively. In contrast, plants exposed to waterlogging at flower initiation generally
exhibited smaller reductions than those waterlogged during the six true leaf pair vegetative
stage. For example, total above-ground dry weight and seed yield in the 3 FI treatment
were reduced by only 32% and 13%, respectively, and seed yield did not differ significantly
from the control.

An overview of the statistical analysis indicated that waterlogging duration and

5. Discussion
In these experiments, hemp, like many other crops, showed poor tolerance to pro-
longed waterlogging, particularly when saturation occurred early in development. Plant
growth and development declined progressively as waterlogging duration increased, re-
gardless of growth stage at which it was imposed. Plants subjected to more than 12 days of
waterlogging at the eight true leaf pair stage did not survive, confirming severe sensitivity
to extended rootzone saturation. In contrast, plants exposed to shorter periods of waterlog-
ging (3–6 days) during the vegetative phase recovered, although recovery was slower after
https://doi.org/10.3390/crops6050090
Crops2026,6, 90 13 of 18
6 days. Despite this recovery, long-term reductions in photosynthesis, growth, dry matter
production, and yield were observed compared with the control.
The physiological impairment underlying these responses is consistent with well-
established effects of hypoxia on root function. Waterlogging restricts oxygen diffusion
and creates hypoxic or anoxic conditions that inhibit root respiration, reduce energy pro-
duction, and impair nutrient uptake. Prolonged oxygen deprivation also causes oxidative
damage to root tissues [3]. Although redox potential (Eh) was not measured in the present
study, complete inundation of the peat-based substrate would have progressively lowered
substrate Eh, creating increasingly reducing conditions. In peat systems, Eh values under
inundated conditions have been reported to be approximately 150–200 mV and may decline
to below 50 mV under strongly reducing conditions [36]. Such low Eh values are associated
with restricted root respiration, altered nutrient dynamics, and reduced plant growth,
providing a quantitative indication of the oxygen-deprived conditions likely experienced
by hemp in this study. These below ground stresses disrupt canopy development and
reduce photosynthesis and yield, as reported in other crops exposed to waterlogging [4–7].
Given the limited comprehensive research on waterlogging tolerance in hemp, com-
parisons with other crops provide useful context. Tolerance thresholds differ widely among
species; wheat and barley can survive and recover from 14 to 16 days of waterlogging
without complete plant loss [37,38], whereas durum wheat shows yield reductions only
beyond 20 days of saturation [ 20]. Cotton, by comparison, exhibits far lower tolerance;
Zhang et al. reported complete mortality in some genotypes after more than 10 days of
waterlogging [39]. In the present study, hemp yield declined by more than 50% after only
6 days of waterlogging, indicating a level of sensitivity comparable to or exceeding that of
cotton and considerably lower than that of most cereals.
Physiologically, waterlogging in hemp caused marked reductions in chlorophyll con-
tent, Pn, Tr, Gs, and F v/Fm, consistent with responses reported for cotton, barley, and
wheat [18,19,21,27,39–41]. Ren et al. have shown that 6 days of waterlogging at the 3-leaf
stage in maize reduced leaf area index, chlorophyll content and photosynthesis, with re-
sponses similar to those observed in hemp after 6 days [42]. Mahmood et al. reported that
7 days of waterlogging in maize reduced chlorophyll content to a degree comparable to the
reduction observed here in hemp after 6 days [43].
Reductions in Tr and Gs were also notable. In hemp, a 15–25% decline occurred
after only 3 days of waterlogging during the most sensitive developmental stage (six true
leaf pair). Comparable responses have been reported in other crops; Kubota et al. (2024)
observed reduced Tr and Gs in soybean after more than 3 days of waterlogging [ 44].
Consistent responses were observed by Ploschuk et al. (2018), who observed declines in
photosynthetic parameters in barley and rapeseed following 14 days of waterlogging at
late growth stages, and in field pea at both early and late developmental stages [40].
Reductions in photosynthetic performance translated directly into losses in biomass
accumulation, TADW, and yield. Although yield declines in hemp were greater than those
commonly observed in cereals, the overall pattern aligns with findings across species: as
waterlogging duration increases, growth and productivity decline proportionally. For
example, Pampana et al. (2016) showed a 19–30% reduction in yield in durum wheat after
40–60 days of waterlogging, whereas hemp in this study exhibited >50% yield loss after
only 6 days of waterlogging, further highlighting its sensitivity [20].
SLA in hemp declined progressively with increasing waterlogging duration. The
greatest reduction (~40%) occurred after 6 days of waterlogging at the eight true leaf pair
vegetative stage in Experiment 1, similar to reductions reported in wheat, barley, and maize
under comparable stress [20,45].

In hemp, the greater sensitivity to waterlogging at the six true leaf pair vegetative
stage compared with the flower initiation stage can be attributed to the importance of early
vegetative growth for plant establishment. During this stage, rapid canopy expansion and
root development create a high demand for oxygen and nutrient uptake. Consequently,
prolonged rootzone saturation may reduce oxygen availability, impair root function, restrict
photosynthesis, and ultimately limit plant growth. Although rootzone oxygen status was
not measured directly in the present study, the observed responses are consistent with those
reported for plants exposed to hypoxic conditions [39,46–48]. In contrast, plants at flower
initiation generally have a more established root system and have already accumulated a
substantial proportion of their vegetative biomass, allowing greater resilience to short-term
stress [39,48]. This pattern differs from crops such as cotton, where waterlogging during
flowering causes the greatest yield loss due to its direct impact on reproductive processes
and boll development [ 21,24]. The contrasting responses reflect crop specific growth
strategies, as hemp yield particularly in fibre types is largely determined by vegetative
biomass accumulation, making early-stage stress more critical than stress imposed during
later reproductive development.
Waterlogging also accelerated hemp phenology, with earlier flower initiation and faster
progression through reproductive stages in severely stressed plants. These findings are
consistent with Zhang et al. (2025) [39], who reported that late stage waterlogging hastened
maturity in rapeseed but differs from other studies showing delayed development in wheat
and cotton. For example, Wang et al. (2017) found that 6 days of waterlogging at the
seedling stage did not affect cotton phenology [21], while Celedonio et al. (2016) reported
that waterlogging delayed flowering and reduced tiller appearance in wheat cultivars [18].
Such variation underscores species-specific and stage-specific responses to waterlogging
and suggests that hemp may shift phenology as a stress-avoidance mechanism under
conditions of prolonged rootzone saturation.
Overall, this study highlights the pronounced susceptibility of hemp to waterlogging
stress and demonstrates that both the duration and developmental timing of saturation
strongly influence physiological function, growth, and yield.
Although these findings are not intended to directly represent field behaviour, they
provide a physiological and stage-specific understanding of hemp responses to transient
rootzone saturation. This information is relevant to controlled environment and nursery
production systems commonly used for hemp and closely related crops, such as medicinal
cannabis, where irrigation management is critical and preventing substrate saturation can
help minimise growth and yield penalties.
6. Future Research Directions
The findings of this study provide a foundation for further investigation into hemp
responses to waterlogging. Future research could incorporate measurements of dissolved
oxygen and substrate redox potential, alongside physiological and growth assessments,
to improve characterisation of the rootzone environment and enhance understanding of
tolerance and recovery mechanisms under waterlogging conditions.
The results reported here are based on a single hemp cultivar (Ferimon 12) and there-
fore may not fully represent the diversity of responses withinCannabis sativa. The high
sensitivity observed in this cultivar may reflect a cultivar-specific response rather than the
full spectrum of waterlogging tolerance available within the species. Future research should
evaluate waterlogging tolerance across a broader range of fibre, grain, dual-purpose, and
dioecious cultivars, as well as southern-adapted landraces, to characterise genotypic varia-
tion in physiological responses, recovery potential, and overall resilience to waterlogging
stress. Such investigations would contribute to the identification of waterlogging-tolerant
https://doi.org/10.3390/crops6050090
Crops2026,6, 90 15 of 18
germplasm and support cultivar selection and breeding for environments prone to transient
flooding events.
The present study focused on the physiological responses of hemp to waterlogging
stress and did not include anatomical assessments. Future studies should investigate
anatomical adaptations associated with waterlogging tolerance, including the formation of
hypertrophied lenticels, aerenchyma, and adventitious roots, as these traits may contribute
to recovery following short term waterlogging. Integrating anatomical and physiological
assessments would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms
underlying waterlogging tolerance and adaptation in hemp.
Future research is also needed to assess fibre quality characteristics, including tensile
strength, fibre composition, and cellulose content. Such analyses would provide a more
comprehensive understanding of the impacts of waterlogging on hemp fibre production
and quality.
7. Conclusions
This study demonstrates that the dual-purpose hemp cultivar Ferimon 12 is highly
susceptible to waterlogging, with both the duration of saturation and the developmental
stage at which it occurs exerting strong effects on physiological function, growth, and
yield. Even short periods of waterlogging during the early vegetative stage (six true
leaf pair) caused lasting reductions in photosynthesis, biomass accumulation, and seed
yield, while prolonged waterlogging resulted in complete plant mortality. Compared with
many cereals, hemp displayed markedly lower tolerance to waterlogging, with 6 days of
waterlogging reducing total aboveground biomass by more than 50% and seed yield by
approximately 48%.
To optimise fibre and seed yield, prolonged waterlogging and the associated rootzone
saturation should be minimised, particularly during early vegetative growth. Successful
hemp cultivation requires well-aerated, free-draining soils or growing media and careful
water management to minimise prolonged soil saturation and the associated risk of reduced
oxygen availability in the rootzone. Although this study was conducted using a single
substrate under controlled glasshouse conditions, the results clearly demonstrate the
importance of waterlogging duration and timing in determining hemp performance.
This work provides a physiological, stage-specific understanding of hemp responses
to waterlogging stress and offers a foundation for improving management strategies in
both field and controlled-environment production. It also highlights the need for future
breeding and agronomic research aimed at enhancing waterlogging tolerance in hemp.
