ABSTRACT
The identification of genetic traits that enable water conserva -
tion could improve water availability for plant use when 
drought may develop. Hemp (Cannabis sativa L.) production in 
the USA has continued to increase dramatically after the federal 
government legalized its cultivation in 2014, but because of 
strict regulations in the past, necessary research for identifying 
adapted cultivars with desirable traits is lacking. The objectives 
of this study were to identify water-saving traits among hemp 
cultivars and determine if they influence cannabidiol (CBD) 
potency. Three approaches were taken: (i) hemp cultivars tran -
spiration rate (TR) responses to soil drying were evaluated in a 
greenhouse, (ii) Sensitivity of cultivars TR to high vapor pressure 
deficit (VPD) was tested in a walk-in chamber, (iii) Field trial data 
were examined to determine if expression of drought-tolerance 
traits was associated with changes in CBD potency. Five out of 
thirteen cultivars had the earliest stomatal closure at rates 
higher than fraction of transpirable soil water (FTSW) threshold 
of 0.55. Four out of twelve expressed the limited transpiration 
rate (TRlim) trait, with their VPD thresholds ranging from 1.2 to 
2.6 kPa. Cultivar Ha3ze showed a desired early decrease in TR 
during soil drying, delayed wilting in the field when soil-water 
deficit developed, and had greater CBD percentage than other 
cultivars. This study provides useful information on variability 
among hemp cultivars in TR response to soil drying and high 
evaporative demand that can be applied in developing cultivars 
that are better suited to a range of water-limited conditions.

the 2018 Farm Bill, which identified hemp as an agricultural commodity. In 
2019, 206,965 hectares were licensed for growing hemp in the USA, which 
was a 554% increase from 31,637 hectares in 2018 (Angell 2019 ).
As a multipurpose crop with three harvestable parts: grain, fiber, and 
flower, the versatility of this plant boosts its popularity, and the potential 
of new markets is appealing to farmers. While traditionally a fiber and grain 
crop, hemp has emerged as a source of non-hallucinogenic medicinal phy -
tocannabinoids (e.g., CBD) (Schluttenhofer and Yuan 2017 ). Because oilseed 
cultivars produce more flowers than fiber cultivars, and the inflorescences are 
where most cannabinoids are produced, these cultivars are a logical source 
for CBD. Moreover, the use of industrial hemp for the production of CBD 
has not received much consideration, although there is commercial interest 
in using field-grown plants for producing CBD (Cherney and Small 2016 ).
The most important physiological process determining crop resource-use 
efficiency is photosynthesis (Tang et al. 2017 ). However, transpiration is 
a natural consequence of stomatal opening to allow CO
2 
to diffuse into the 
interior of the leaf to be assimilated in photosynthesis. Hence, transpirational 
water loss is necessary and quantitatively linked to plant growth and produc -
tion (Tanner and Sinclair 1983 ).
To overcome the negative impacts of drought stress in crops, many 
strategies have been investigated and adopted, mainly including agronomic 
management and genetic improvement (Kebede et al. 2014 ). The physiolo -
gical traits that appear to be particularly desirable to improve performance of 
crops under water-deficit conditions are generally associated with limited 
water use early in the growing season so that water is conserved for use later 
in season to sustain plant productivity during seed fill (Manandhar et al. 
2017 ; Shekoofa and Sinclair 2018 ).
Studies have shown that partial stomatal closure in response to both 
progressive soil drying and high atmospheric vapor pressure deficit (VPD) 
can confer water-saving properties on plants because of reduced plant tran -
spiration rate (TR) (Shekoofa et al. 2013 ; Devi et al. 2014 ; Sinclair et al. 2018 ). 
Therefore, plants expressing one or both stomatal closure traits can conserve 
soil water and prolong crop physiological activity under dry conditions.
The first response to a leaf water deficit is stomatal closure (Hura et al. 2007 ), 
which occurs before any change in relative water content (RWC) occurs. 
According to Wanjiku et al. ( 2019 ), RWC can be used to monitor water status 
of the plant during growth and under drought stress. RWC is percent of water at 
a given time as related to the water content at full turgor (Gonzalez and 
Gonzalez-Vilar 2001 ).
While substantial progress has been made in identifying varieties of peanut 
( Arachis hypogaea ), maize ( Zea mays ), sorghum ( Sorghum bicolor ), cowpea 
( Vigna unguiculata ), and soybean ( Glycine max ) that possess drought- 
tolerance traits, little or no attention has been given to hemp cultivars. Since

2. Materials and methods
In two controlled-environment studies [transpiration rate (TR) response to 
progressive soil-drying and TR response to varying evaporative demand (i.e., 
vapor pressure deficit)], two different sets of hemp cultivars were tested. The first 
set included eight hemp cultivars known to be grain and fiber cultivars. 
The second set included six oilseed/CBD cultivars received as transplants from 
the University of Tennessee’s Department of Plant Pathology. The same six 
oilseed/CBD cultivars were tested in a field experiment during summer 2019.
For the progressive soil-drying study, each set was tested simultaneously 
across a period of eight weeks. For the TR under high evaporative demand 
test (growth chamber study), space was limited; therefore, only two cultivars 
were tested at a time during the course of 12 weeks.
2.1 Progressive soil-drying study
The progressive soil-drying tests were conducted in a greenhouse at the West 
Tennessee AgResearch and Education Center (WTREC) in Jackson, 
Tennessee, in two sets of experiments. Air temperature was set for 32 day/ 
26 ⁰C night. During the course of the study, the temperature and relative 
humidity were measured every five minutes using a universal serial bus 
(USB) data logger (MicroDaq, Contoocook, NH).
In set 1, the hemp seeds of eight cultivars, viz., “Finola,” “Kurd,” “Joey,” 
“Felina,” ‘CFX-2 ʹ , “Delores,” “Canda,” and ‘CFX-1 ʹ (for grain and fiber), were 
initially sown in commercially available planting cubes (Jiffy 50-Cell Tray, 
Lowe’s, Jackson, TN) filled with potting mix (Miracle-Gro). Cultivar ‘CFX-1 ʹ 
had very poor germination; therefore, it was dropped from the experiment. 
Seeds were planted 1-cm deep, with five seeds cube

before transplanting to 5.7-liter pots filled with soil containing 50% sand and

50% silt. In set 2, six additional oilseed/CBD cultivars, viz., “Ha3ze,” “Carolina,” 
“T1 Topped (T1 T),” “T1 Not Topped (T1 NT),” “Cherry Topped (Cherry T),” 
and “Cherry Not Topped (Cherry NT),” which were already in vegetative growth

For set 1, three seedlings per pot were transplanted into nine replications per 
cultivar (a total of 117 pots). After two weeks, the plant-stand was thinned to one 
plant per pot. The plants were grown for 22 days under well-watered conditions, 
watered each day to return to pot capacity, and fertilized once a week using 
Maxi-Gro (General Hydroponics) nutrient with 10-5-14 (N-P-K). Plants were 
still in a vegetative stage at this point. If flowers appeared, they were excised and 
set on the top of the pot to remain in the weight calculation, but prevent any 
reproductive growth and extra transpiration.
The day before initiation of the soil-drying experiment, all pots for both 
set 1 and set 2 were watered until there was drainage from the bottom of each 
pot. Eight plant replications were kept per cultivar. The following morning, 
each pot was sealed into two double-bagged 15-liter plastic bags (Glad, 15- 
liter white trash bag, Walmart, Jackson, TN) and secured at the base of the 
plant with plastic twist ties. A plastic tube (13-mm-diam. × 126-mm-long) 
was also inserted to facilitate watering. All pots were weighed immediately 
after bagging, and these measurements constituted the initial weights. 
Each day, the pots were weighed between 13:00 and 14:00 hours, Central 
US Time. Daily transpiration was calculated as the difference in weight of 
each pot on successive days (Shekoofa et al. 2013 ).
After the second day of these measurements, the plants received water based 
on their TR for that day. Three pots per cultivar were selected to be “well- 
watered check,” and the other five received the soil-drying treatment 
(“stressed”). The well-watered plants were watered daily to maintain a weight 
of no less than 200 g of their initial weight, whereas each of the other five plants 
was allowed to dry through transpiration. To achieve an extended drying period 
and to prevent rapid dehydration, the drying pots were watered daily when 
necessary to maintain daily net water loss to no more than 100 g (Sinclair et al. 
2018 ). This process was continued across a two-week period.
The TR data were subjected to two normalizations, as described by Shekoofa 
et al. ( 2013 ). The first normalization was to minimize the influence of large 
variations in daily TR across days. The daily transpiration ratio for each drying 
pot was calculated between its water loss and the average water loss of the three 
well-watered pots. The second normalization was done to account for variation 
in transpiration ratio among individual plants. The daily transpiration ratio was 
divided by the mean transpiration ratio of that same plant during the first three 
days of the study, when the soil still had high water content. This ratio was 
identified as the normalized transpiration ratio (NTR), and its value during the

[PAGE 6]
initial wet phase of the dry-down cycle for each plant, by definition, was centered 
on a value of 1.0.
Data collection for each pot continued until it dried to an NTR ≤0.1, which 
was defined as the endpoint of transpirable soil water. The total transpirable soil 
water available to the plant in each pot was calculated as the difference between 
the initial weight and the final weight of the pot. To track soil drying, FTSW was 
determined on each day for each pot. The FTSW of each pot was calculated as 
the difference between daily and final weight, divided by the total transpirable 
soil water. The use of transpirable soil water as the basis for comparing plant 
response to soil drying under a range of conditions has been effectively done in 
a few studies (Ray and Sinclair 1997 ; Serraj et al. 1999 ). Data for plants of 
a cultivar were combined to regress NTR on FTSW.
The data were analyzed using a two linear-segment regression analysis via 
GraphPad Prism version 8 (GraphPad Software). This regression analysis 
generated the FTSW threshold for the initiation in the decline in NTR. The 
95% confidence interval of the threshold for each cultivar was used to assess 
statistical differences among cultivars (Sinclair et al. 2018 ).
2.2 Growth chamber study under varying evaporative demand
Two sets of experiments were conducted in this study. First set, eight hemp 
cultivars; “Finola,” “Kurd,” “Joey,” “Felina,” ‘CFX-2 ʹ , “Delores,” “Canda,” and 
‘CFX-1 ʹ , were germinated from seeds and grown in a greenhouse at WTREC 
Jackson, TN. For the second set, four additional oilseed/CBD cultivars; 
“Carolina,” “T1 T,” “T1 NT,” and “Cherry T” were received as transplants 
from the University of Tennessee’s Department of Plant Pathology.
The plants were grown in pots constructed from polyvinyl chloride pipe (10- 
cm diameter and 20-cm long). The bottom of each pot was fitted with a flat end 
cap, in which a small hole was drilled to allow drainage of excess water. A toilet 
flange was attached to the top of the pot to allow easy attachment of a VPD 
chamber during measurements (Fletcher, Sinclair, and Allen 2007 ; Shekoofa 
et al. 2015 ). The pots were filled with commercially available Miracle-Gro 
potting mix. Five seeds per pot were sown. After one week, the plant-stand 
was thinned to three plants per pot, and after 10 days, it was reduced to one plant 
per pot. Maxi-Gro (General Hydroponics) with 10-5-14 (N-P-K) was applied 
once a week. Plants were grown for 28 days under well-watered conditions, with 
greenhouse temperatures regulated at 32 day/26 ⁰C night.
Once plants reached the desired growth stage (considering shoot/root ratio), 
they were moved to a walk-in growth chamber and overwatered until water 
started dripping from the bottom of each pot. Aluminum foil was placed around 
the base of each plant, covering the soil to seal in all moisture and prevent soil 
evaporation. Then the pots were allowed to drain overnight (Shekoofa et al. 2015 ).

To construct each VPD chamber, a 340-mm diameter food container lid 
(Cambro Manufacturing, Huntington Beach, CA), with the center cut out, 
was attached to the toilet flange at the top of the pot. The following morning, 
the aerial parts of each plant were enclosed in a 21-L clear plastic food 
container (Cambro Manufacturing, Huntington Beach, CA) by placing the 
inverted container over the plant and attaching it to the previously installed 
lid. Each VPD chamber was fitted with a 12-V, 76-mm-diameter computer 
box fan (Northern Tool and Equipment, Burnsvillle, MN) to continuously 
stir the air inside the chamber. In addition, a temperature/humidity data 
logger (MicroDaq, Contoocook, NH) was mounted through the sidewall of 
each container to measure the chamber environment.
Three levels of VPD were achieved using air flowmeters and controlling 
the source of the air (ambient or dehumidified). During each of these levels 
of VPD, TRs were measured. The temperature was kept at a constant 32 ⁰C 
comparable to the greenhouse environment. Each day was begun by attaining 
low VPD (0.5–1.5 kPa), then escalating to medium VPD (1.5–2.5 kPa), and 
finally reaching high VPD (2.5–3.5 kPa). After achieving the target VPD 
level, the chamber was allowed to stabilize for 30 min, and then the entire pot 
chamber system was weighed to the nearest 0.1 g to obtain the initial weight. 
The plants were then exposed for one hour to each VPD level and then 
reweighed to measure the final weight. TR at each VPD level was calculated 
as the difference between the initial weight and the final weight.
Eight plants were studied at a time, allowing two cultivars with four 
replications. To increase the data points taken on each plant, measurements 
were done on each plant across two consecutive days. After completing 
measurements on the first day, the pots were watered and allowed to drain 
overnight. After completing the second day of measurements, leaves were 
harvested and total plant leaf area was measured using a leaf area meter (LI- 
3100, Li-Cor, Lincoln, NE). Finally, the TR for each plant was expressed as 
water-loss rate divided by plant leaf area.
Data from the two measurement days for all plants of a cultivar in each 
experiment were combined to perform a two-segment linear regression (Prism 
8.0, GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA) for TR vs. VPD. When significantly 
different (p < 0.05) slopes for two segments were not obtained for a cultivar, 
a simple linear regression was applied to all the data. For those cultivars expressing 
the two-segment response, the regression analysis yielded the VPD breakpoint 
between the two linear segments as well as the slope of each segment. The standard 
error for the VPD breakpoint and the two slopes was an output of the regression 
analysis. The term breakpoint is used to define the VPD (kPa) point at which the 
plant begins to close stomata partially and conserve the water remaining in the soil.

2.3 Field study
A field study was conducted at a West Tennessee field in Beech Bluff, TN, 
during summer 2019. Sixteen CBD cultivars were transplanted into the field 
in the third week of June 2019. All the cultivars were received as transplants 
from Dr. Eric Walker, Genamera, Springfield, TN. Each cultivar was repli -
cated three times, totaling 48 plots in the field. The trial was established using 
a randomized complete block design with six replications. Each plot con -
tained two rows of 10 plants spaced 1.2 m apart and rows were spaced 1.8 m 
apart. Although, because of limited availability of hemp transplants, only six 
cultivars, viz., “Carolina,” “Cherry T,” “Cherry NT,” “Ha3ze,” “T1 T,” and 
“T1 NT,” from the controlled-environment studies were available to be 
considered for the field observation.
During the growing season, soil fertility was managed by applying a per hectare 
total of 219.5 kg N, 165.5 kg P, and 125.5 kg K preplant. Weeds were managed by 
cultivation throughout the season. The total rainfall was higher than 30-year 
average in early June but lower than average in July and August, and much 
lower than average in September. Temperatures were similar to 30-year average 
during each month, except September, where temperature was 7–9% higher.
Samples for RWC and specific leaf area (SLA) were taken 63 and 91 days 
after transplant (DAT) between 11:00 am and 3:00 pm, when the ambient 
VPD and temperature were up to 4.2 kPa and 37 ⁰C, respectively. Following 
sampling methods described by Gonzalez and Gonzalez-Vilar, to measure the 
RWC and SLA, the youngest but fully expanded leaf from the middle part of 
the plant was removed and placed in a sealed plastic bag. The sampler would 
blow into the bag and put samples on ice in the field to ensure that the 
samples remained fresh and maintained turgidity. Then, each plant was 
visually assessed for wilting and assigned a score based on severity of wilting, 
0 = no wilting and 5 = the plant essentially senesced.
Samples were taken back to the lab where their fresh weight was measured (g) 
and then immediately after, leaf area was measured with a leaf area meter (LI- 
3100, Li-Cor, Lincoln, NE). The following day, the leaflet samples were placed in 
a tub of deionized water for 6–8 h and exposed to 4 ⁰C in a walk-in cooler before 
weighing once again for a turgid weight. Leaflet dry weights were measured after 
placing the leaflets in individual paper envelopes (coin envelopes, Uline.com) 
and placed in an oven at 80 ⁰C for 12 h. After all measurements were taken,

Hemp plants were hand-harvested at maturity by cutting the plants at the 
base, then immediately bucked to remove flower and leaf material from the 
stem. Flower and leaf material were then dried on mesh screen shelves in 
temperature- and humidity-controlled rooms. Drying times varied by variety; 
weights were recorded after drying. All plants in each plot were harvested, 
but the actual number of plants harvested per plot varied because of differ -
ences in plant availability from suppliers and plant survival. Because of this, 
plot weights were divided by the number of harvested plants and yields are 
reported on a kg per plant basis. Dried material was sent to Altitude Lab 
Solutions (Englewood, CO) for analyses of cannabinoid potency using high 
performance liquid chromatography (HPLC).
The value of max active CBD concentration was determined for each plot 
and used to calculate CBD yield in kg per plant. Finally, values for hemp 
physiological parameters were compared among cultivars using one-way 
ANOVA. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05. The treatment means 
were separated using Tukey’s HSD test (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).
3. Results and discussion
3.1 Progressive soil-drying study
The two-segment linear regression successfully described the transpiration 
response rate to soil drying of all hemp cultivars ( Table 1 ). There were 
substantial differences among the cultivars. The FTSW threshold for cultivar 
Delores was 0.81 ( Table 1 ). Cultivar Delores had the earliest stomatal closure 
and highest water saving under soil-drying conditions, whereas Canda with 
an FTSW threshold of 0.16 ( Table 1 ) had stomatal closure last and maximum 
water loss early during growth ( Table 1 ). Among all cultivars, the correlation 
coefficient for the two-segment regression ranged between 0.44 and 0.98 
( Table 1 ). The diverse FTSW thresholds for the tested hemp cultivars support 
the observations of Devi et al. ( 2009 ), in which there was large variation 
among 17 peanut genotypes in a progressive soil-drying study. Sinclair et al. 
( 2018 ) reported significant differences in the FTSW threshold in each of the 
tested peanut populations. Among the 22 North Carolina State University 
breeding lines tested, FTSW thresholds ranged from 0.38 to 0.69.
Across six hemp cultivars (T1 NT, T1 T, Cherry T, Cherry NT, Carolina, 
and Ha3ze) tested in the field, there was a negative but significant correlation 
between low wilting scores (WS) observed in the field and high FTSW 
thresholds (i.e., early stomatal closure) in the controlled environment

3.3 Field study
The natural daily range of evaporative demand in the field allowed measure -
ments to be made under ambient VPD up to 4.2 kPa and temperature up to 37⁰C 
while plants experienced rainfed conditions the entire season. The increase in 
midday temperature resulted in progressively higher evaporative demand 
through the day. Therefore, all visual wilting scores were recorded between 
11:00 a.m. and 1:00 p.m. Cultivars Ha3ze, Carolina, and T1 T had the lowest 
wilting scores ( Figure 1 ). Furthermore, cultivars Ha3ze, Carolina, and T1 T had 
significantly higher RWC rates compared to Cherry T ( Figure 2 ). Figure 3 
depicts a negative correlation ( p = 0.05) between FTSW threshold in the con -
trolled environment and wilting scores recoded in the field for the tested hemp 
cultivars ( Figure 3 ).

5. Conclusion
Soil water conservation for hemp can be a very important factor; therefore, 
identifying drought-tolerance traits (i.e., early stomatal closure under soil 
drying and TR
lim
) is critical for improving hemp production. This research 
demonstrates that variation among hemp cultivars for the drought-tolerance 
traits should help in identifying superior cultivars for rainfed environments. 
The expression of the drought-tolerance traits was generally characterized 
similarly under both controlled environments and field conditions. 
Consequently, the results of this study that showed stability in the expression 
of the early stomatal closure in progressive soil-drying conditions should 
accelerate future efforts toward comparing germplasm for the water-saving 
trait under a range of conditions.
