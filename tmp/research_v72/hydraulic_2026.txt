Abstract
Industrial hemp ( Cannabis sativa L.) is versatile and rapidly developing, offering new
prospects to producers as a multipurpose crop, yet limited water availability in the Mediter-
ranean area due to climate change makes its sustainable management challenging. Al-
though the plant’s water requirements have been studied, a significant gap remains regard-
ing irrigation management based on the hydraulic properties that govern water movement.
The present study elucidates the role of soil hydraulic parameters in water dynamics within
the rhizosphere of industrial hemp ( Cannabis sativa L., ‘Felina 32’). For this purpose, a
pot experiment of three irrigation treatments (100% FC, 80% FC, 60% FC; FC is the field
capacity) was set up using two different soil types (clay loam CL and sandy clay loam
SCL). SCL soil showed a higher Cmax of about 4 cm−1 compared to the Cmax of 0.11 cm−1
of CL soil, but dropped drastically within a narrow frame of soil moisture. CL soil resulted
in about 12-fold higher diffusivity (Dmax ≈ 0.23 cm2 min−1) within a wider range of soil
moisture compared to the SCL soil (Dmax ≈ 0.02 cm2 min−1), which facilitated water re-
distribution at CL, allowing the plant to maximize its water uptake, even at the lowest
water input. As a result, the CL soil allowed more flexible scheduling and in contrast,
SCL soil necessitated a high frequency irrigation protocol. The integration of hydraulic
properties into irrigation planning revealed the potential of CL to apply water to plants
efficiently across full and deficit irrigation, showing the peak performance of the irrigation
water use efficiency (IWUE) (0.929 g/mm) under the 60% FC regime. The findings provide
a framework for linking soil physics–agricultural hydraulics with irrigation strategies in
controlled environments.
Keywords: agricultural hydraulics; soil physics; soil–water interaction; precise irrigation;
hydraulic capacity; hydraulic diffusivity; soil water characteristic curve (SWCC); irrigation
water use efficiency (IWUE)
1. Introduction
Over the last few years, Mediterranean and semi-arid regions have often suffered
from water scarcity due to climate change. The impact of climate change on crop selection
is big, as farmers are increasingly turning to more drought-tolerant crops. Lately, more
farmers have given their attention to industrial hemp ( Cannabis sativa L.), a crop that is
widely known for its pharmaceutical characteristics, along with cloth production ability and

biofuel [1]. One of the main advantages of hemp cultivation is the moderate to low input
requirements, making it an important crop for sustainable agriculture [ 2–5]. Moreover,
the Felina 32 variety has a low tetrahydrocannabidiol (THC) level and is usually used for
fiber [6,7]. Thus, Felina 32 has attracted many researchers to investigate its response to
various hydraulic conditions, along with the air and soil temperature, soil contamination
and climate conditions for either fabric or bioenergy use [8–10].
Water is the most vital water resource; however, its scarcity due to the climate crisis
restricts crop productivity. In recent years, hazards have been increasing, as rainfall more
often leads to floods, while on the other hand, droughts are expanding. In areas where water
is scarce, such as the Mediterranean, sustainable water resource management combined
with crop production is a critical challenge and a key solution for the future [11,12]. Crop
productivity is connected with the texture and aggregation of the soil [ 13], while the
soil’s hydraulic properties play a decisive role in irrigation planning and practices [14–19].
Hydraulic properties such as hydraulic conductivity, diffusivity, capacity, infiltration rate,
etc., are linked to irrigation planning and scheduling and play a vital role in influencing
plant behavior under different irrigation regimes. In addition, hydraulic properties are
crucial for the horizontal and vertical movement of the irrigation water, with a great impact
on the irrigation dose and duration [ 20]. Among the crucial hydraulic properties, field
capacity (FC) is the ideal value for soil moisture for crops [11] and due to its importance,
the present research uses FC as the upper level of soil moisture, while irrigation treatments
were designed as FC ratios. FC varies according to the soil texture, because the clay, silt
and sand percentages of the soil directly affect the water retention and, therefore, the water
availability, which in turn regulates the physiological characteristics, such as height and
leaf development, which are linked to biomass accumulation [21].
Lately, several researchers focused on water-consumption-related factors, such as crop
coefficients (Kc) and crop evapotranspiration (ET c), along with the agronomic response
of the crop to the water deficit and temperature [21–23], but there is still a significant lack
of investigation into the effect of the hydrodynamics of the soil–water dipole, along with
the hydraulic properties, on irrigation decisions and hemp’s behavior. The response of
industrial hemp to controlled hydro-stress remains under investigation, particularly when
the texture, structure and hydraulic properties are factors under consideration. Moreover,
as deficit irrigation enhances the irrigation water use efficiency (IWUE) with simultaneous
water resource conservation [ 2,4,22], controlled water stress may play a key role in the
cultivation of industrial hemp in areas where droughts have become increasingly frequent
and water scarcity is the harsh reality. To that direction, considering the hydraulic properties
along with the soil’s physicochemical characteristics, we can achieve precise and efficient
water application with a simultaneous undamaged yield.
The objective and innovation of the present study is to elucidate the role of hydraulic
properties in governing water dynamics in the root zone of industrial hemp. By integrating
high-resolution hydraulic properties such as the hydraulic capacity (or specific water ca-
pacity, specific moisture capacity, or differential water capacity) C(θ); hydraulic diffusivity
D(θ); hydraulic conductivity K(θ), including van Genuchten’s model parameters; SWCC
and infiltration into irrigation decisions, this research highlights how soil physics and agri-
cultural hydraulics are connected to irrigation strategies. The study provides a mechanistic
framework for understanding the role of soil water redistribution and hydraulic behavior in
water management. Thus, this research intends to contribute to the current understanding
of irrigation water management by presenting an additional perspective that promotes
crop protection and the efficient use of water resources. A containerized cultivation system
was intentionally selected to ensure a controlled hydraulic environment, which allowed for
the monitoring of the water balance, avoiding deep percolation and runoff, minimizing the

2. Materials and Methods
2.1. Experimental Design
The experiment was implemented in Velestino, Greece, at the farm of the University
of Thessaly (39 ◦23′44′′ N, 22◦45′26′′ E, alt. 70 m) during the Spring–Summer season of
2022. Two soil types were used: one taken directly from the farm (soil A) and the other
(soil B) was a mixture of sand and farm soil in a 1:10 ratio. Thirty pots were filled with
both soil types (half filled with soil A and the remaining half filled with soil B). Industrial
hemp (Cannabis Sativa L., ‘Felina 32’) seeds were planted in 20 L volume pots, each of
which was filled with 10 L of soil. To prevent seeds from microbiological impact, before
seeding, NaClO was used for ten minutes over the seeds [6]. Sowing followed the rule of
10 seeds per pot and the plants were later thinned to three per pot. The experimental design
consisted of 2 soil types (A, B) × 3 irrigation treatments (I1, I2, I3) × 5 replications = 30 pots.
During the experimental period, one plant with the B-I1 treatment showed symptoms
of disease, leading to its exclusion. To ensure a strictly balanced experimental design,
we standardized the dataset to 4 replicates per treatment. Irrigation water was applied,
consisting of 3 irrigation treatments, as follows: I1: 100% FC (0% residual) defined as the
control, I2: 80% FC (20% residual), and I3: 60% FC (40% residual). Water was applied using
the spaghetti tube irrigation system and the flow rate was 10 mm/h. The soil moisture
was monitored daily by Delta-T SM 150. The plant’s quantitative characteristics (length
(L), biomass (m) and dry biomass (dry m)) were measured, using a measuring scale and a
digital weighing scale. At each pot, the average of the three plants was recorded (for the
characteristics under research (L, m and dry m)), and the average of the replications of
each treatment was also recorded. Figure 1 presents the experimental procedure during
two representative stages of plant growth.

2.2. Soil Physicochemical Properties
The soil samples were taken to the laboratory and dried in the oven at 105 ◦C, and the
soil characteristics have been analyzed [13,26–28]. The samples were sieved using a 2 mm
pore sieve and underwent chemical analyses to estimate the pH, the electrical conductivity
(EC), the organic matter (OM), the CaCO3 and the soil particle distribution [6,28,29]. The
soil pH and electrical conductivity (EC) were assessed in a soil:water (1:1) mixture; the
organic matter content was measured using the Walkley Black method, using K 2CrO4
as an oxidizing factor. The content of CaCO 3 was measured by a proper calcimeter and
the particle size distribution of the soil was estimated by the Bouyoucos method. In the
experimental procedure of the current study, the same original soil (A) and soil ratio (B)
with [13] were used.
2.3. Hydraulic Properties
The soil samples were delivered to the laboratory to measure the following
hydraulic properties:
• Field capacity (FC).
• Permanent wilting point (PWP).
• Soil moisture at saturation (θs).
• Bulk density (ρb).
• Cumulative infiltration [I(t)] and infiltration rate [i(t)].
• Soil water characteristic curves [SWCC h(θ)].
The van Gencuhten equation [30] was used to simulate the SWCC:

and n are parameters which have to do with the shape and the curvature of the SWCC. The
Kostiakov [31] equation was used to determine the cumulative infiltration functions for
both soils being researched.
For the determination of FC, PWP , and SWCC, the experimental device used at the
laboratory was a pressure plate apparatus (Soil Moisture Equipment Corp., Goleta, CA
USA), while a digital weighing scale was used to calculate θs. The soil moisture thresholds
(FC and PWP) were determined by applying 0.33 bar and 15 bar tensions to the saturated
soil samples, correspondingly. For the determination of the SWCC, the tension range
was 0.05 to 15 bars for soil A (CL) and 0.04 bar to 15 bars for soil B (SCL). Infiltration
was measured by using transparent columns of 50 cm height and 6 cm inner diameter,
measuring the incoming water at certain time intervals. RETC v. 6.02 software was used
to simulate the SWCC and the hydraulic properties of hydraulic conductivity K( θ) and
hydraulic capacity C(θ), which are equal to a derivative of soil moisture in terms of suction
dθ/dh and hydraulic diffusivity D(θ), which are equal to K(θ)/C(θ).

4. Discussion
Based on their respective classifications, clay loam (soil A) and sandy clay loam (soil
B) showed distinct physical behaviors: soil A’s finer texture allowed for greater water
storage than soil B, which is a coarser texture soil. This behavior is reflected in the hydraulic
properties of the two soils. Soil A showed greater soil moisture in saturation, residual
moisture, FC and PWP , while bulk density and hydraulic conductivity were significantly
lower. The difference between the soil textures was also captured at the cumulative
infiltration curves and the infiltration rate curves (Figures 3 and 5), where at early times of
infiltration, soil B showed a greater rate, as expected [18,32]. The infiltration rates of both
soil samples were fundamental for the determination of the irrigation system used during
the experiment. Soil A exhibited an initial infiltration rate of 2.5 mm/min (150 mm/h),
reaching a steady state of 0.5 mm/min (30 mm/h). Soil B showed higher infiltrability,
with initial and final values of 3.29 mm/min (197.4 mm/h) and 0.6 mm/min (36 mm/h),
respectively. In order to avoid ponding, overflow and runoff, flow rate was set to 10 mm/h,
considering the hydraulic behavior of soil A, providing a safety margin for both soil types.
As infiltration is strongly influenced by both the soil texture and hydraulic parameters, it is

For this purpose, a deeper investigation into the hydraulic parameters has been achieved.
The SWCC has been designed for both soils and was approximated with van Genucthen’s
equation. Using the experimental suction–moisture pairs, more hydraulic parameters
have been obtained, which was affected by the soil moisture, capturing the hydrodynamic
behavior of each soil. In particular, the hydraulic capacity, hydraulic diffusivity, hydraulic
conductivity, and pressure head behavior with the soil moisture was captured, showing that
differences in soil texture significantly affect the irrigation-related hydraulic properties [37].
The above was considered to use water precisely in the irrigation system. Clay loam
showed greater field capacity, while its SWCC showed greater availability of water for the
plants (Figure 4). Thus, the selected MAD, equal to 0.6, guaranteed that irrigation was kept
within the limits where soil moisture remained viable for the plant. The selected MAD
ensured hydraulic security at soil B too (Figure 6), preventing sharp increases in suction
that would bring enormous stress to the plant.
Hydraulic capacity C(θ) revealed the buffering capacity of CL soil, as the curve was
significantly “smoother” than the one of SCL soil (Figures 4 and 6). The smaller slope of
C(θ) indicates that soil A was less sensitive to moisture changes, signifying smoother water
supply to the plant. Hydraulic capacity is defined as dθ/dh, meaning that the sharp drop
of C(θ) in soil B (Figure 6) indicates that as soon as the plant absorbs a small amount of
water, the suction rises sharply, making the remaining water difficult to access [37,38]. More
specifically, soil B shows a higher Cmax of about 4 cm−1 compared to the Cmax of 0.11 cm−1
of soil A, but drops drastically within a narrow frame of soil moisture. Thus, the hydraulic
capacity derived from van Genuchten’s model served as a proxy for the hydraulic buffering
ability of each soil. Moreover, C(θ) curves indicated that soil A revealed a more stable state
for the water content than soil B, which justifies the decision for less frequent irrigation
intervals at soil A, as it had the ability to supply the plants with the required amounts of
water with fewer changes in suction, preventing transient water stress (Figures 7 and 8).
The hydraulic capacity analysis indicated that in the soil moisture range, the peak value,
along with the shape of the curve, is a key point for irrigation management. The distinct
slopes and the narrow or wide peaks of the curves provide a mechanistic explanation
for the soil’s retention ability and water availability to plants. Soil A acted as a reliable
hydraulic buffer, supporting plants even at 60% FC (I3) treatment. On the contrary, soil B
with the sharp curve of hydraulic capacity could provide water within a narrow range of
soil moisture, demanding more intensive and careful water management. Such hydraulic
behaviors can explain the observed differences in IWUE and the peak of A-I3 treatment.
Also, the sharp hydraulic capacity of soil B demonstrated likely induced transient water
stress, resulting in lower IWUE compared to soil A across all irrigation levels.
Figures 4 and 6 also provide the hydraulic diffusivity curves for both soils, indicating
that as diffusivity is defined as D(θ) = K(θ)/C(θ), the impact of both hydraulic conductivity
and capacity on the hydraulic diffusivity is significant [ 30]. As a result, soil A showed
significantly higher diffusivity than soil B, indicating that the water profile was more
uniform and steadier compared to soil B, where the lower values of D( θ) mean that the
water movement was physically constrained, as a result of the soil’s texture, structure
and hydraulic behavior [ 30,37]. Moreover, the significant difference in IWUE between
the two soils is mathematically justified by the relationship between D(θ), K(θ) and C(θ),
confirming the benefit of the soil physics–agricultural hydraulics perspective. Hence, soil
A resulted in about 12-fold higher diffusivity (D max ≈ 0.23 cm2 min−1) within a wider
range of soil moisture compared to soil B (Dmax ≈ 0.02 cm2 min−1), which facilitated the
water redistribution of soil A within the pot, allowing the plant to maximize its water

infiltration rate, causing water to penetrate more rapidly while being retained less in the soil

plants and the fluctuations of the soil moisture. In addition, the diffusivity levels resulted in
faster, but less stable, water movement in the rhizosphere, increasing the risk of unevenness
and the need for more frequent irrigation events. Thus, irrigation management in the

required values, but also reduced the irrigation water use efficiency (I1: 0.452 g/mm, I2:
0.560 g/mm, I3: 0.746 g/mm) due to quick infiltration, indicating that sandy clay loam
requires more careful water management. The performance of the soils in terms of the
IWUE is directly linked to their hydraulic characteristics. The maximization of the IUWE
in soil A under the I3 regime highlights the optimal utilization of water resources when
considering hydraulic properties. The steeper curve of C( θ), along with the narrow soil
moisture range, the lower diffusivity and the higher infiltrability of soil B, is responsible for
the intense scheduling of water application and the lower efficiency of the irrigation water,

and IWUE [11,42,43]. As a result, the clay loam soil offered greater flexibility, allowing
for fewer, but more efficient irrigation applications, while the sandy clay loam required
stricter and more frequent planning to meet either the crop needs or the reduced crop needs
as planned. Despite the different strategies, the results showed that the three irrigation
regimes of full and deficit irrigation did not significantly affect the productivity of Felina
32 in terms of height, yet the fresh and dry biomass were affected, as expected [ 7,9,39].
However, the increased irrigation water use efficiency in clay loam at 60% FC demonstrates
that the rational combination of hydraulic properties with adapted irrigation strategies can
achieve both production maintenance and water resource conservation [43–46], notably for
applications in textile/fabric production.
The findings of the present study suggest that irrigation planning based on hydraulic
properties reveals the potential of significant improvement of IWUE, highlighting the
value of precision irrigation in environments with limited water resources, such as the
Mediterranean. The findings are a proof-of-concept of how soil physics and agricultural
hydraulics determine the sustainability of industrial hemp. The present study focuses on
the research of inherent hydraulic properties in a controlled hydraulic environment, in
order to monitor the water balance and simultaneously avoid deep percolation and runoff,
and also minimize the spatial heterogeneity of the soil. It is important to mention the
limitations of this research though, which refers to the certain variety of industrial hemp
(Felina 32), the certain soil types, the containerized environment and the Mediterranean
conditions of central Greece, while additional repetitions of the experiment in future work

5. Conclusions
The main scope of the present research was to integrate hydraulic properties into the
irrigation practices of industrial hemp (Felina 32). For this purpose, two different soil types
were used to investigate the impact of the different texture and hydraulic behavior of each
soil on irrigation scheduling in full and deficit irrigation treatments, along with the effect
on the irrigation water use efficiency. The main finding of the study is the mechanistic link
between hydraulic properties and irrigation. Moreover, irrigation management based on
hydraulic properties led to the following key findings:
• Irrigation system, optimal flow rate and MAD selection: infiltration analysis led to a
select flow rate at a safety level below the final infiltrability in order to avoid ponding
and runoff. SWCC and diffusivity provided the basis for the MAD selection and the
spaghetti irrigation system, respectively.
• Hydraulic properties and irrigation decisions: Decoding the hydraulic behavior of
each soil revealed their different ability for providing water to plants. The combined
analysis of hydraulic capacity C(θ), hydraulic conductivity K(θ) and diffusivity D(θ)
facilitated the separation of the irrigation strategy, as the soil moisture range of the
curves and the peak value and the shape of the curves was a key point for irriga-
tion management. As a result, the soil physics–agricultural hydraulics perspective
of irrigation management allowed for more flexible scheduling for clay loam soil,
due to its higher ability to retain and provide the available water to plants linked
to the wider curve of C( θ), the higher D( θ) values, the lower K( θ) and the wider
range of soil moisture. In contrast, sandy clay loam, due to its abrupt hydraulic
behavior, necessitated an intensive and more careful irrigation management, as it
showed a sharp C(θ) curve, lower D(θ) values and higher K(θ) in a narrower range of
soil moisture.
• Impact on irrigation water use efficiency: Irrigation based on hydraulic properties
highlighted the ability of clay loam to efficiently apply water to Felina 32 across
full and deficit irrigation. The rise in IWUE with the decrease in water applied is
directly linked to the soil’s specific hydraulic properties, connecting soil physics and
agricultural hydraulics with peak agronomic performance.
While the present research was conducted in a controlled containerized environment,
the integration of hydraulic properties into irrigation decisions provides a mechanistic
framework for future field-scale applications.
